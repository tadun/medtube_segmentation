Blood collection tubes are among the most widely used single-use medical plastics: a large hospital processes tens of thousands per day, yet virtually all are incinerated alongside hazardous clinical waste. This linear-economy outcome persists because manual sorting is impractical at scale---even capped tubes carry contaminated exterior surfaces from centrifuge and processing environments, exposing workers to unacceptable infection risk. Closing the loop to a circular economy requires an automated pipeline: tubes are \emph{segregated} by cap type, \emph{opened} to separate cap from body, \emph{cleaned} and \emph{sterilised}, then returned to \emph{reuse} or single-polymer recovery streams. The tubes considered here are single-use polypropylene and polyethylene plastics in a controlled laboratory setting; their partially transparent, specularly reflective surfaces make purely colour-based classification unreliable under varying clinical lighting---a central motivation for the RGB-D approach taken in this work.

The target deployment is a conveyor-belt sorter in which an inclined feed channel presents one tube at a time to the overhead classification station, with a downstream pneumatic air-jet actuator directing each tube to the correct stream. The inclined geometry guarantees single-tube presentation and matches the one-tube-per-session data collection protocol used throughout. The production-line target uses the SR2000 industrial scanning head, which demands sub-25\,ms per-frame latency. All experiments reported here are performed on static images captured in a controlled laboratory environment, establishing accuracy and speed baselines before dynamic validation at belt speed once the conveyor hardware is commissioned. This work is the vision component of a larger system that also includes Jessiah Buamah's reinforcement-learning air-jet controller.

A dataset of 3\,000 paired RGB and depth images was captured with an Intel RealSense D415 depth camera and annotated across four cap-closure classes (Universal, Push-on, Screwcap, Other) using SAM\,3-assisted polygon labelling on Roboflow. Five YOLO-family instance segmentation models were evaluated across RGB, RGBD, and depth-only modalities. Adding a depth channel to a compact 2.8\,M-parameter model (YOLO11n-RGBD, mask mAP\textsubscript{50--95}\,=\,0.929) outperformed a network ten times larger trained on RGB alone (YOLOv8m-seg, 0.905); YOLO26n-seg delivered the best speed--accuracy trade-off at 23.1\,ms per frame. A depth-only model reached 0.804, confirming that tube geometry is discriminative and inherently robust to the reflective, transparent surfaces that confuse RGB classifiers. A live inference pipeline demonstrates real-time classification at 30\,fps, ready for integration with the conveyor-belt sorting hardware.
